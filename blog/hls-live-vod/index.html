HLS 直播与点播：m3u8 的两种用法 HLS Live vs VOD: Two Uses of M3U8

Last updated: September 19, 2026

2026-08-28 · 5 min read

一、同一个 m3u8，两种模式

很多人以为 m3u8 只是“点播文件”，其实它既能做点播（VOD）也能做直播（Live）。区别不在文件后缀，而在清单里的“时间轴是否还在生长”。

二、点播（VOD）

清单是完整且固定的——所有分片一次性列全，末尾有 #EXT-X-ENDLIST 标记。播放器知道总长、能拖动进度条、可以任意跳转。普通影视、课程、录播都用这种。

三、直播（Live）

清单持续增长：服务端不断往末尾追加新分片、删除旧分片，#EXT-X-ENDLIST 不存在（或更晚才出现）。播放器定期刷新清单、追着最新的分片播，所以是“跟着时间走”。直播延迟主要来自分片时长与缓冲区。

四、怎么一眼区分

  • 打开你的 m3u8（或粘贴到 在线播放器），看是否含 #EXT-X-ENDLIST。
  • 有 → 点播；没有且画面一直在更新 → 直播。
  • 直播还会带 #EXT-X-MEDIA-SEQUENCE 标明当前窗口的起始序号。

五、给开发者的提示

直播断流时，往往不是“地址失效”，而是服务端停推了——此时清单还在但分片拉不到。这也是为什么 播放失败排查要先区分“地址失效”和“CORS 被拦截”。

1. Same m3u8, two modes

Many think m3u8 is just "VOD", but it does both VOD and Live. The difference isn't the extension but whether the timeline is still growing.

2. VOD

The playlist is complete and fixed — all segments listed at once, ending with #EXT-X-ENDLIST. The player knows the total length, supports seeking and arbitrary jumps. Movies, courses, recordings use this.

3. Live

The playlist keeps growing: the server appends new segments and drops old ones; #EXT-X-ENDLIST is absent (or appears much later). The player periodically refreshes and chases the newest segment, so it "follows time". Latency comes mainly from segment length and buffering.

4. How to tell at a glance

  • Open your m3u8 (or paste into the online player) and look for #EXT-X-ENDLIST.
  • Present → VOD; absent but picture keeps updating → Live.
  • Live also carries #EXT-X-MEDIA-SEQUENCE marking the start index of the current window.

5. A note for developers

When a live stream drops, it's often not a dead URL but the server stopped pushing — the playlist remains but segments can't be fetched. That's why the troubleshooting guide first separates dead URLs from CORS blocks.

6. There are actually three modes, not two

Most explanations stop at live and VOD. The specification also defines EVENT, and knowing the difference saves a lot of confusion when a stream behaves oddly:

An EVENT playlist is the honest answer when you want people to join late and rewind to the start, but the broadcast has not finished yet.

7. Where live latency actually comes from

Latency is not one number, it is a sum. From camera to screen it roughly adds up as:

end-to-end latency ≈ (segments buffered × segment duration)
                   + encoder and packaging delay
                   + network and CDN transfer time

The first term dominates, and it is the one you control. A player that keeps three segments in the buffer is deliberately sitting three segment-lengths behind the edge:

Buffer arithmetic for a player holding three segments
Segment durationApproximate added latencyPractical result
10 s (older HLS default)~30 sVery stable, hopeless for interaction
6 s (common today)~18 sStandard broadcast feel
4 s~12 sAcceptable for most events
2 s (LL-HLS partial segments)~6 sNear real time
LL-HLS with preload hints2–5 sComparable to WebRTC

Cut the segment length and you cut latency, but you also multiply the number of requests every viewer makes and give the encoder less room to compress efficiently. Every low-latency setup is a trade between those three.

8. How to measure your own latency

You do not need special tooling — the "two clocks" method works:

  1. Open a page showing an accurate clock, such as time.is, on your computer.
  2. Start the live stream on your phone and point the phone camera at the screen.
  3. The gap between the clock in the video and the clock in the photo is your glass-to-glass latency.

If you are building the player, hls.js exposes it directly: hls.latency gives the distance behind the live edge, and hls.targetLatency / hls.liveSyncPosition let you tune how close to the edge the player dares to sit.

9. LL-HLS in one paragraph

Low-Latency HLS keeps the same playlist model but slices segments into partial segments that can be published before the whole segment exists, plus preload hints that tell the player what to request next. It brings HLS into the 2–5 second range without abandoning HTTP caching. The catch is that the whole chain — encoder, packager, CDN and player — has to support it; one link that does not, and you fall back to regular HLS latency.

Frequently asked questions

Can I watch a live stream from the beginning?

Only if the server keeps the old segments. In a rolling-window live playlist they are deleted as new ones arrive, so the earliest you can seek is the start of the window. In an EVENT playlist, or once the stream is republished as VOD, the whole recording is seekable.

What is the DVR window?

The span of time a live stream keeps available for rewinding — typically a few minutes to a few hours, depending on how many segments the server retains. It is a server-side decision, not a player feature.

Does #EXT-X-MEDIA-SEQUENCE ever reset?

It increases monotonically as the window slides and should not go backwards. If you see it jump backwards, the packager restarted and players will usually treat the stream as broken and rebuffer.

Why is my stream 30 seconds behind when the segment is only 6 seconds?

Because your player is holding several segments in the buffer. That is intentional — each extra buffered segment buys resilience against a brief network stall, and costs one segment length of latency.

Is lower latency always better?

No. Chasing sub-second latency shrinks the buffer that protects you from jitter, and a stream that stalls every thirty seconds is worse than one that is fifteen seconds behind. Match the latency to the use case: interactive betting needs seconds, a concert does not.
